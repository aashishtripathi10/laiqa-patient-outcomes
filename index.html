1Executive summary

Laiqa operates a clinician-supported programme for women's hormonal and metabolic health (PMOS, formerly PCOS; hypothyroidism; perimenopause; related conditions), delivered through personalised protocols and follow-up consultations. This report consolidates what the programme's clinical and product records show about the population served and the outcomes documented to date.

Payment-agnostic protocol

Free-trial and unpaid users receive the same first-line protocol as paying users; prescription frequency in clinician notes is comparable across paid and unpaid groups. The documented difference is follow-up duration and consultation cadence, not the first-line protocol itself. This parity evidence is prescription-frequency, not a full plan-depth audit.

Programme description (summary)

Care is personalised through a driver-based protocol engine that treats root-cause drivers (insulin, cortisol, inflammatory/androgen, gut, thyroid) rather than disease labels, with documented decision rules, sequencing and conflict resolution. Delivery is multi-modal — diet, lifestyle, herbal and medical modalities — under clinician oversight. The full description is in Appendix B. This section describes the operating model; it is not evidence of efficacy.

Measurement instruments

App-tracked Menstrual-cycle dates

Period start/end dates logged in the app by 763 users. Cycle-length standard deviation computed from the recorded cycle dates. No clinician interpretation involved. Used in §4.

Lab / imaging Before/after panels

HbA1c, prolactin, AMH, SGOT/SGPT, vitamin D, uric acid and ultrasound imaging drawn from lab reports attached to consultations. A curated set of ~29 cases; selected, not a random sample. Used in §5.

Clinician-documented Symptom notes

Clinicians recorded which concerns were present or resolved at follow-up visits. This is the basis of the symptom-resolution data (§4). Subject to documentation variability and follow-up selection.

Self-report Questionnaires (B2B)

MWQ (Menstrual Wellness) and LQ (Lifestyle Quotient) — Laiqa-administered self-report questionnaires, delivered before/after in two employer cohorts. They embed some established sub-scales (Perceived Stress Scale, SATED sleep), but the composites are not externally validated or published. Used in §6.

Outcome capture in the historical cohort used clinician documentation and app-tracked logs rather than a single standardised scored instrument. A scored composite instrument (the PMOS Health Score) has been designed but did not measure the historical cohort in this report; see Appendix A.

4Core clinical outcomes

Two outcome domains are presented: app-derived menstrual-cycle regularity (descriptive) and clinician-documented symptom resolution (observational). Neither is an efficacy estimate from a controlled trial.

App-derived longitudinal outcome: menstrual-cycle regularity

Regularity is defined as a cycle-length standard deviation under 5 days, the programme's pre-specified criterion. Cycle length is the gap between consecutive logged period-start dates, filtered to 10–120 days.

7Clinical safety & governance

This section summarises what the documentation supports about clinical governance and safety. It describes the design and the current limitations; it does not claim a level of governance that cannot be demonstrated.

Documented governance design

  • Clinician involvement: protocols are delivered under named clinicians (Ayurvedic medicine practitioners registered under NCISM, operating as registered medical practitioners under telemedicine norms), with per-instance clinician approval for medicines and supplements.
  • Red-flag screening: an intake red-flag screen (e.g. TSH > 50, myxoedema flags) precedes protocol assignment.
  • Safety gates and escalation: documented hard-rule escalation triggers (e.g. HbA1c ≥ 10, TSH > 10, self-harm flags) route to immediate clinician handoff; clinicians can take over any case. The number of times these triggers have fired is not separately tracked.
  • Medication/supplement controls: individual medicines and supplements require clinician sign-off before they reach the member.

Regulatory status

Clinical delivery is by Ayurvedic medicine practitioners registered under NCISM (National Commission for Indian System of Medicine), operating under the Telemedicine Practice Guidelines. The scope and basis of the practitioners' registration, and the regulatory classification of the protocol engine and of any supplement or herbal products administered, are matters of record held by the clinical team; their formal classification under drug or consumer-safety frameworks is a diligence follow-up rather than a settled position here. The precise application of telemedicine and product-safety frameworks to this delivery model has not been the subject of a formal regulatory determination, and this report does not represent that it has been. Supporting practitioner registrations, SOPs and product documentation are available for diligence review.

Adverse-event handling and current limitations

Adverse events that have surfaced to date include: a member reporting continuous bleeding for ~3 months after the protocol period; a 10-day bleed following an induction agent (Rajapravartini Vati), which triggered a diagnostic workup; a case of symptom worsening ("stress — no improvement, in fact increased"); and a mid-plan hospitalisation for dengue (not protocol-caused) that forced a pause. Procedure-related risk — for example Virechana (therapeutic purgation) or herbal preparations taken alongside existing medication — is noted as not systematically tracked.

Current adverse-event capture is not systematic, so the observed incident count should not be interpreted as an incidence rate. The programme has protocol-level safety gates (approval, escalation, clinician takeover), but systematic adverse-event capture and tracking is not yet in place.

8Limitations

The main limitations of the current dataset, stated plainly.

  • Historical cohort, not the current delivery product. The outcome data in this report describe the earlier clinician-led programme (before 7 Apr 2026). The current WhatsApp-based product is a separate delivery model; no outcome dataset is presented for it in this report, and the generalisability of the historical cohort to the current product is not established.
  • No control / comparator arm. Improvements cannot be causally attributed to the protocol; cycle and skin conditions also change naturally.
  • Observational and heterogeneous endpoint capture. Endpoints are documented by treating clinicians at follow-ups, with variable follow-up selection and documentation style; there is no single standardised instrument over the historical cohort.
  • Clinician assessment, not independent adjudication. The clinicians who delivered the protocols also recorded the outcomes; results are not independently validated or blinded.
  • Missing data and follow-up decay. 1-month endpoint capture was 85.2% (92 of 108 starters); 3-month and 6-month follow-up logging fall to 50% and 30% respectively. The by-month improvement series is subject to survivorship (see Appendix D).
  • Selected objective cases. The lab/imaging cases are clinician-selected and do not represent cohort-level rates; imaging was not standardised; co-medication was not fully documented.
  • Self-report instruments. MWQ/LQ composites are not externally validated; some sub-scales are established (PSS, SATED).
  • Retention context. Lifetime paid renewal is 8.1% of paid B2C users (77 of 946) — a different denominator (all paid B2C users on record) from the strict-paying cohort of 894 B2C in §2, and a different metric from the coded renewal-reason analysis.
  • No systematic adverse-event register. Incident counts should not be read as incidence rates.
  • No controlled efficacy study. A registered controlled trial has not yet been conducted; the current data are real-world evidence, not proof of controlled clinical efficacy.

9Bottom line

Laiqa has accumulated a substantial real-world dataset across clinician consultations, longitudinal patient records, patient-reported outcomes and selected laboratory/imaging observations.

Clinician-documented symptom resolution was recorded for several high-frequency concerns among patients with documented follow-up, with completion-adjusted estimates providing a conservative view of endpoint capture. Selected laboratory and imaging cases provide additional objective evidence, but these cases are clinician-selected and do not establish cohort-level efficacy.

On the objective cycle data, a proportion of trackable users currently have regular cycles, but the available within-user analysis does not yet demonstrate that Laiqa causes cycle regularisation.

The evidence base is observational, heterogeneous and lacks a controlled comparator. It therefore supports Laiqa's real-world clinical operating model and provides a meaningful evidence-generation asset, but does not establish causal treatment efficacy.

The principal evidence-generation priorities are systematic longitudinal outcome capture, systematic adverse-event tracking and prospective controlled evaluation.

AData sources & methodology

A.1 Cohort definitions

The full treated population (1,428 users with usable clinical signal) includes paid, unpaid and free-trial users, Jun 2024 → Jun 2026. 859 users had ≥1 clinician consultation; 763 logged period cycles; 947 were strict paying patients (894 B2C + 77 B2B − 24 both). The historical cohort described by the outcome sections is the pre-restructure clinician-led programme (before 7 Apr 2026); the current WhatsApp-based product is referenced separately where noted.

A.2 Denominator definitions

Each statistic carries its own denominator. For the symptom-resolution data, three related counts coexist: 180 = patients with any logged 1-month endpoint (the per-completer denominator), 108 = one-month-plan starters, and 92 = the 108 starters who had the endpoint logged (the 85.2% capture rate). Symptom-resolution rates are reported as the share of patients with the concern who had a documented 1-month follow-up endpoint, with per-symptom denominators shown in §4. Weight-cohort figures use the clinician-defined "valid trends" denominator (n=136). Cycle-regularity figures use users with ≥3 or ≥4 logged cycles (n=90 / n=45).

A.3 Endpoint definitions

  • Symptom resolution: a concern recorded at baseline and no longer recorded at the 1-month check-in, as marked by the treating clinician.
  • Cycle regularity: cycle-length standard deviation < 5 days, computed on valid intervals (10–120 days) between consecutive period-start dates.
  • Weight loss: member-logged weight recorded lower at the end of the tracked period than at baseline, within the clinician weight-loss cohort.
  • Questionnaire outcomes: before/after MWQ and LQ scores (self-report).

A.4 Follow-up windows

Symptom resolution is measured at the 1-month check-in. Follow-up logging across the cohort: 85.2% at 1 month, 50% at 3 months, 30% at 6 months. The by-month improvement series runs to 4 months with shrinking n (see Appendix D).

A.5 Missing-data handling

The completion-adjusted estimate multiplies the per-completer resolution rate by the overall observed 1-month endpoint-capture rate of 85.2% (92 of 108 one-month-plan starters). Patients with no usable weight trend are excluded from the weight cohort (n=136 of a larger treated set).

A.6 Completion-adjusted calculation methodology

The completion-adjusted estimate multiplies the per-completer resolution rate by the overall observed 1-month endpoint-capture rate of 85.2% for each symptom cluster. This is a modelling adjustment based on an assumption of uniform endpoint capture across symptom clusters; it is not a patient-level missing-as-unresolved analysis and is not a conventional clinical-trial ITT analysis.

A.7 Objective-case selection methodology

The ~29 lab/imaging cases were selected by clinicians from cases with paired before/after laboratory or imaging observations attached to consultations. They are selected positive cases and are presented as case evidence, not as cohort-level estimates.

A.8 Questionnaire methodology

MWQ and LQ are Laiqa-administered self-report questionnaires delivered before and after the programme in the Deloitte (n=150, 2-month) and Advatix (n=24, 12-month) cohorts. Composites are not externally validated; some sub-scales (Perceived Stress Scale, SATED sleep) are established instruments. Results are self-reported and subject to selection effects (e.g. MWQ base 38 of 150 at Deloitte).

A.9 Data-source provenance

Data are drawn from Laiqa's clinical/product systems: consultation records and clinician notes (~2,885 consultations; 4,295 notes), app-logged cycle dates, intake and follow-up documentation, lab reports and ultrasound findings attached to consultations, and questionnaire results from the two employer cohorts. Aggregate figures were validated against the underlying production records on multiple occasions (most recently Jun 2026).

A.10 Limitations

See Section 8 of the main report. In addition: severity scores are banded (57% of scores at "9"), and a prior internal cycle-regularity figure of 64% could not be reproduced from current data (ceiling across eight definitions 57.8%) and has been retired.

BProgramme description

Care is personalised through a driver-based protocol engine that treats root-cause drivers — insulin, cortisol, inflammatory/androgen, gut, thyroid — rather than disease labels alone. Each member is assigned one primary and one secondary driver from a signal-weighted assessment; a 2-of-3 signal cluster guards against false positives, and lab values override symptoms where relevant.

  • Disciplined timeline: observe-only week 1; earliest layering at day 14; primary-driver changes only at day 28 and only on high compliance with zero improvement. Compliance zones: ≥80% clear / 50–79% clinician judgement / <50% simplify.
  • Conflict resolution: documented sequencing for all driver pairs, with a safety-first tiebreaker order.
  • Healing arcs: pre-specified outcome timelines set expectations (e.g. for stress-driven amenorrhea, cycle regulation is expected last, at 8–12 weeks, not 2).

This describes the operating model and is not evidence of efficacy. The protocol-level safety gates (red-flag screen, per-instance approval, escalation triggers, clinician takeover) are summarised in Section 7.

Clinician weight-loss cohort

In the clinician weight-loss cohort, 52% (71 of 136) of members with valid logged weight trends lost weight, at a reported average of ~2.03 kg/month. The per-user distribution and measurement setting are not documented in full; the average is among those who lost. Concurrent medication was not systematically documented for these members, so co-treatment cannot be excluded. This is directional real-world evidence, not a clinic-measured figure.

Biomarker case-selection note

The lab/imaging cases in Section 5 were selected by clinicians from cases with paired before/after results. They include imaging-confirmed changes (fatty liver, ovarian morphology, cyst size) and the AMH/prolactin pair. Labs were not standardised across cases (same-lab/same-assay/fasting/time-of-day not documented per case), and imaging was not standardised.

EEvidence references

External peer-reviewed literature used in the design of the protocol, and the internal evidence categories. External citations support biological and protocol rationale; they do not establish Laiqa-specific efficacy.

External evidence

  • Macronutrient- and seed-cycling protocols cite Varady & Hellerstein 2007; Harvie & Howell 2017; Paoli 2012; Paddon-Jones 2008 (AJCN reviews).
  • Myo-inositol carries published PCOS evidence; used in the womb-healing protocol.
  • CBT for amenorrhea RCT: 87.5% resumed ovarian activity vs 25% observation-only — backs the mind-body/stress arm.
  • The stress × emotional-eating × sleep mechanism (cortisol/insulin drivers) is grounded in Dweck et al. 2014, Appetite (3-way interaction F=5.52, p=.022).

Internal evidence categories used in this report

  • Internal clinical evidence: clinician-documented observations from Laiqa's own patient population (symptom resolution, follow-up notes).
  • Objective case evidence: selected laboratory/imaging observations (Section 5).
  • Product / engagement evidence: app-tracked or patient-reported longitudinal data (cycle regularity, retention, questionnaires).

Internal Laiqa evidence is useful real-world evidence but is not equivalent to independently replicated clinical evidence.
